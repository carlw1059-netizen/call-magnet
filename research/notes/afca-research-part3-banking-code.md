## 3. Banking Code of Practice 2025 — Hardship and Vulnerability Obligations

### 3.1 Overview and Commencement

The **2025 Banking Code of Practice** (BCOP 2025) was approved by ASIC on **27 June 2024** following an extensive ASIC-led consultation, and came into force on **28 February 2025**. [1] It is the first substantive revision since 5 October 2021.

The ABA's member banks — including CBA — are contractually bound by the BCOP. Its provisions form part of the contract between a bank and its customers. This is critical to how the Code interacts with AFCA determinations (see Section 3.4 below).

ASIC Chair Joe Longo stated the review was "focused on ensuring this Code can make a difference in the day-to-day practice of the banking sector and through that, good customer outcomes." [1]

---

### 3.2 Strengthened Hardship Obligations

**Expanded definition of Financial Difficulty**

The 2025 Code introduces a broader definition of "Financial Difficulty" than the 2021 version:

| 2021 BCOP | 2025 BCOP |
|---|---|
| Inability to repay due to an unexpected event or unforeseen changes outside control | Inability *or difficulty* to meet current **or future** repayment obligations, due to illness/injury, loss of employment, pandemic, natural disaster, or other reasons |

The expanded definition is deliberately broad ("or other reasons") and captures future payment difficulty — not just current inability to pay. The purpose, per the ABA, is to "encourage more customers to seek help in times of distress, before their financial condition irreversibly deteriorates." [2]

**Key hardship obligations under the 2025 Code:**

1. Banks must **proactively identify** customers in financial difficulty — not wait for the customer to formally trigger the hardship process
2. Banks must **respond to hardship requests** within mandated timeframes and provide written notice of any decision, including when declining assistance
3. Banks must offer **individually tailored responses** — not standardised "cookie cutter" replies
4. Banks must refer customers to **free external support services**, including financial counselling and, with customer consent, mental health support [3]
5. Banks must not take enforcement action (default notices, debt recovery) against a customer while a hardship request is under assessment or a matter is before AFCA

**ABA Financial Difficulty Guideline (July 2025):** The ABA released an updated voluntary industry guideline on 1 July 2025 for banks' financial difficulty programs. While voluntary, it reflects best practice expectations and is broader than the NCC obligations (covering small businesses and guarantors as well as consumers). It was released alongside the 2025 Code to guide implementation. [4]

### 3.3 Strengthened Vulnerability Obligations — Mental Health

The 2025 Code significantly strengthens the vulnerability framework. The revised definition of vulnerability explicitly lists characteristics that may increase a customer's vulnerability risk: [3]

- Mental health conditions
- Cognitive impairment
- Serious medical conditions
- Disability
- Age (particularly older customers and young people)
- Elder abuse
- Family and domestic violence
- Financial abuse
- Financial difficulty
- Literacy and/or language barriers
- Cultural background
- Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander customers
- Remote locations
- Incarcerated persons

Critically, the 2025 Code establishes that **any customer can become vulnerable at any time** — vulnerability is not a fixed status but a dynamic condition. [2]

**New and strengthened obligations regarding vulnerable customers:**

1. **Identification obligation:** Banks must have systems and processes capable of identifying customers who may require extra care — and this extends to frontline staff at the first contact point, not just specialist teams.

2. **Response obligation:** Where a customer discloses circumstances suggesting vulnerability (including mental health), the bank must adjust its approach accordingly. The standard is not passive acknowledgment but active accommodation.

3. **External referral obligation:** Banks must now, with customer consent, refer customers to free external support services including:
   - Financial counselling
   - **Mental health support**
   - Help for customers impacted by family or domestic violence
   - Interpreter services [3]

4. **Contact management:** Where a customer has appointed an authorised representative or requested that contact go through a third party, the bank must comply with that direction. Continuing direct contact after such a disclosure is a breach of both the Code and banking practice standards.

5. **Documentation:** Banks must document vulnerability disclosures and the accommodations made in response. Failure to document is itself evidence of inadequate process.

**ABA Customer Vulnerability Guideline (November 2024):** The ABA published this guideline to give practical guidance on implementing the vulnerability obligations. It identifies mental health as an explicit trigger for extra care processes and sets out best practice for identification, response, and escalation. [5]

### 3.4 How Code Obligations Interact with AFCA Determinations

This is a frequently misunderstood area. The interaction operates through two distinct channels:

**Channel 1 — Contractual breach**

The BCOP is incorporated into the contract between a bank and its customers. CBA is an ABA member and a Code subscriber. Where CBA breaches the BCOP, it has breached its contractual obligations to the customer. AFCA can determine that this breach caused the complainant loss (financial and non-financial) and require the bank to remedy it.

AFCA's published factsheet on Code obligations confirms: *"Where a credit provider does not comply with the Code to which it is a subscriber, then the financial firm is likely to have breached its obligations to its customer."* [6]

**Channel 2 — AFCA Rule A.14.2**

Under AFCA Rule A.14.2, AFCA may have regard to relevant provisions of industry codes when determining what is fair in the circumstances of a complaint — even for firms that are not subscribers. For Code subscribers (including CBA), this gives AFCA authority to treat Code obligations as the applicable standard against which the bank's conduct is measured.

**Are Code breaches independently determinative?**

No — a Code breach is not automatically determinative of the outcome in an AFCA proceeding. AFCA assesses each complaint holistically, considering:
- The specific facts and evidence
- What remedy is fair in all the circumstances
- Established legal principles (causation, remoteness, mitigation)

However, in practice, a Code breach by a subscriber bank carries substantial weight:
- It confirms the bank had a specific obligation that it did not meet
- It is treated as a breach of the bank's contractual duty to the customer
- It directly supports an award of compensation (financial and non-financial) where causation is established

The distinction between "independently determinative" and "evidential" is therefore somewhat academic for subscribers. A Code breach does not automatically produce an award, but it is strong — often decisive — evidence that the bank fell short of its obligations.

**Precedent from AFCA determinations:** Published banking determinations confirm that AFCA routinely finds that a bank's failure to comply with BCOP obligations (particularly vulnerability and hardship provisions) *"did not align with the Banking Code of Practice"* and uses that finding as the primary basis for requiring NFL compensation. [7]

### 3.5 Specific Code Provisions: Contact, Referral, and Documentation

**Maintaining contact with distressed customers:**

The 2025 Code requires banks to:
- Maintain proactive communication with customers in hardship — the bank must not become unreachable or unresponsive
- Provide clear timelines for hardship decisions and communicate delays
- Assign a dedicated case manager or consistent point of contact for customers in hardship (best practice, supported by ABA Guideline)

**Referral to hardship assistance:**

Banks must:
- Tell customers about the bank's hardship program at the earliest opportunity when signs of financial stress appear
- Provide clear information about what assistance is available (payment deferrals, restructuring, interest-only periods, term extensions)
- Refer customers to free external financial counsellors (the National Debt Helpline and equivalent services)
- Not require customers to "fail" the standard repayment process before triggering hardship assessment

**Documentation requirements:**

Banks must document:
- All hardship requests received (regardless of channel — phone, online, branch)
- The circumstances disclosed by the customer
- The assistance offered and the outcome of any negotiation
- Written notice of any decision to decline hardship assistance — this is a mandatory requirement under both the BCOP and the National Credit Code

AFCA's systemic investigation found **228 instances** at one firm where mandatory written decline notices were not issued between 2022 and 2024. This volume — 228 separate customers who did not receive notice of their declined hardship request — illustrates how documentation failures are systemic, not accidental. [8]

### 3.6 BCCC Compliance Findings

The Banking Code Compliance Committee independently monitors Code compliance and publishes bi-annual reports.

**January–June 2024 report:** The BCCC reported an increase in breaches of key Code obligations during this period. The report underscored "the need for banks to strengthen oversight and processes to better support customers, particularly customers experiencing vulnerability and financial difficulty." [9] Banks provided financial remediation to customers for 29% of reported Part 3 breach incidents.

**2024–25 annual report (published August 2025):** The BCCC found "tangible improvements" in how banks serve vulnerable customers following its 2021 inquiry follow-up. However, it also:
- Concluded investigations into deceased estate handling, finding Code breaches and securing **$4 million+** in customer remediation [10]
- Noted stronger internal oversight at banks and clearer pathways for vulnerable customers — but acknowledged further improvement is needed
- Launched an improved breach reporting model in 2025 focused on high-risk issues

**Lending identified as leading source of breaches:** The most common Banking Code breach category in recent BCCC data is lending obligations — including hardship response requirements — ahead of other areas. [11]
